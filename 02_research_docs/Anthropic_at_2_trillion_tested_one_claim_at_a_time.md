Anthropic at $2 trillion, tested one claim at a time

Anthropic is preparing to list on the stock market at a reported price of about $2 trillion, roughly 31 times the revenue it earns in a year at today's pace. This report asks what a buyer at that price is being asked to believe, and checks each belief against the evidence.

It's written for someone who knows the basics of investing and of AI, and no more. Every chart is rebuilt from the numbers in the source named under it. Where a figure comes from an investor's opinion rather than a filing or dataset, it says so.

52 parts. Sources sit under each one. Financial information, not financial advice. Written with Claude, which is made by Anthropic; that's a reason to hold every claim here to a higher standard, and the sources are there so you can.

1
What $2 trillion asks you to believe

A share is a claim on a company's future profits. Pay $2 trillion for Anthropic and you're making three bets at once. First, that revenue keeps growing at a pace no company has sustained before. Second, that a business which today keeps about 44 cents of every dollar after paying for computing can one day keep 70. Third, that customers who can switch AI models in an afternoon will stay and keep paying more. Each of the sections that follow tests those three bets against what's actually on the record.

Sources: IPO target of about $2 trillion and a raise of up to $100 billion: Reuters, via PYMNTS, Sep 2026. Gross-margin estimate: PitchBook's Harrison Rolfes, Morningstar, Sep 4, 2026.

5
Three pillars, and what each one needs to reach

Every argument in this report lands on one of three pillars. Growth is the bet that $65 billion becomes the $190–200 billion Anthropic has projected for 2028. Profitability is the bet that the margin after computing costs rises from an estimated 44% to about 70%, the level ordinary software companies enjoy. Durability is the bet that customers stay, and it's the only pillar with no headline number yet, because retention and customer concentration will be disclosed for the first time in the IPO filing. At $2 trillion the price is 31 times this year's revenue pace and about ten times the 2028 projection.

Section 1 of 5

Is Claude actually the best?

The growth pillar starts here. Businesses pay Anthropic a premium because they believe its models are better. This section checks that belief against independent leaderboards, a clean benchmark, and one real production codebase, and then asks how long a lead can last when rivals copy it in months.

6
Why the company with the most chips usually wins

In 2019 the AI researcher Rich Sutton wrote a short essay called "The Bitter Lesson." His point, drawn from seventy years of chess, Go and speech recognition, was that methods that learn from data and scale with computing power keep beating methods built on human cleverness. That's why every frontier lab spends most of its money on chips. Epoch AI estimates Anthropic spent more than ten times as much on computing in 2025 as two leading Chinese labs combined. A separate study found the computing needed to reach a fixed level of ability halves roughly every eight months, so the same money buys more each year, for everyone.

Sources: Sutton, "The Bitter Lesson," 2019. Compute comparison: Epoch AI, Apr 2026. Eight-month halving: Ho et al., arXiv 2403.05812.

The tasks Claude can finish alone doubled every four months

METR, a research group that tests AI systems, measures how long a task a model can complete on its own, in hours of skilled human work. In early 2025 Anthropic's best model managed tasks of about an hour. By early 2026 Claude Opus 4.6 handled tasks of roughly twelve hours, and the Mythos preview released in April went beyond sixteen. The doubling time is about four months. This is the clearest evidence that the "acceleration" story is true so far, and it's why the AI labs talk about models helping build the next model. It's also a reminder that any lead measured this way is a moving target.

The best free model sits 12 points behind, about four months back

"Open-weight" models can be downloaded and run by anyone, and the best of them now come from China. Epoch AI, which tracks capability over time, finds the best open models trail the best closed ones by about four months, a gap that hasn't widened or closed much. Anthropic releases no open models. 11
On a real codebase, the cheaper model needed three times the repairs

Leaderboards measure test tasks. One study measured production work. A developer ran two 28-day periods on the same company codebase: first Claude Opus 4.7 and 4.8 through Claude Code, then a Chinese open model, GLM, running on the company's own Nvidia hardware. With prompt caching, Claude's effective cost fell to 57 cents per million tokens, below the $2.83 the company's own hardware cost per million. And the open-model setup generated far more rework: three-quarters of its commits were fixes, against under half for Claude. This is one developer and one codebase, so treat it as a case study. It's the best evidence we have that "good enough and cheaper" can cost more once you count repairs.

Section 2 of 5

Who pays for the chips

Anthropic owns almost none of the computers it runs on. It rents them, on borrowed money, from companies that are also its shareholders and rivals. This section follows the money from the cloud giants' spending plans through to the interest rates lenders charge on Anthropic's name, because profitability is decided here.

A quote to set up the section

"somebody has to pay for it, right?"

Brad Gerstner, Altimeter Capital, on the trillion-dollar-plus data-centre build-out. All-In Summit, 2026 (project transcript).

Gerstner's fuller argument on stage was that Microsoft, Google and Amazon aren't building data centres for themselves. They build them to rent out, and the AI labs are the tenants. If the labs' revenue doesn't grow fast enough to cover the rent, the building has to stop. He put a number on it: the three biggest labs together earn about $100 billion a year and, in his view, need to reach at least $180 billion by the end of 2026 to keep the whole AI trade intact. The next two slides check his premises against filings and independent data. The $180 billion is his judgment, and it's labelled that way.

A frontier model can die before it pays for itself

Epoch AI studied the economics of OpenAI's GPT-5, the best public case. Over its four months as the flagship it earned about $2 billion of gross profit, revenue minus computing. Money is available. Nearly $50 billion of debt was raised for Anthropic's build-out when its run rate was under $9 billion. The price of a million tokens fell 41% in six months

AI is sold by the token, roughly three-quarters of a word. Ramp tracks the effective price businesses pay per million tokens across all models. It peaked at $1.15 in March 2026 and fell to $0.68 by early September, a 41% drop, after price cuts by both OpenAI and Anthropic. Frontier models' share of tokens fell from 53% to 45% as companies set cheaper standard models as their default. Do the arithmetic: if price falls 41%, volume has to rise more than 70% just to hold revenue flat. Anthropic's growth is now a volume story, and the volume is going to its cheaper models. The price war is self-inflicted, and it's already here.
