How Much of the World's
$40 Trillion in Knowledge
Work Can Anthropic
Actually Get Paid For?
A first-principles essay on the $2 trillion question, using Anthropic's S-1 prospectus filed on 26
October 2026.


  Overview. In short, the question is how much of the world's $40 trillion in knowledge work
  Anthropic can actually collect as revenue. I break it into four parts. How big the prize is,
  whether AI is paid for like software or like labour, what rivals cannot copy and who else
  takes a cut, and whether the money adds up once computing costs are counted. For each
  part I ask a chain of "why" questions, keeping only the answers that hold up against the S-1
  and outside data. Then I rebuild an answer from what survives, which turns out to be a few
  hundred billion dollars, if four conditions hold.




Why This Question
On 26 October 2026 Anthropic filed its S-1, the document a company must publish before
selling shares to the public. NPR reported that the listing could value the company at $1.5 trillion
to $2 trillion.

The prospectus itself frames the opportunity as knowledge work, which it says created more
than $40 trillion of economic value worldwide in 2025.

Most debates stop at "is $2 trillion a bubble?" That question is too loose to answer. A sharper
one asks how much of that $40 trillion Anthropic can actually collect as revenue. By our rough
estimate, a $2 trillion valuation needs something like $300 billion of yearly sales at maturity.

That is under 1% of the $40 trillion. So the size of the prize is not the problem. The problem is
who else gets paid first.

To work through it I use the first-principles method from Farnam Street. It has four steps. State
the question, break it into parts, ask a chain of "why" questions that test each assumption (this is
the Socratic part), and then rebuild an answer from what survives.

Anthropic describes the industry as a race to superintelligence. I treat that as the company's
own framing, not as a fact, and look only at who pays whom.

Takeaway. The prize is not the problem. Who gets paid first, and for how long, is.


Step One. Break the Question Into Parts
The question splits into four parts.

  ●​ How big is the prize, and how fast is Anthropic growing into it?
  ●​ Is AI paid for like software, with a fee per user, or like labour, with a fee per job done?
  ●​ What does Anthropic own that rivals cannot copy, and who else takes a share of each
     dollar?
  ●​ Does the money add up once the cost of computing power is counted?

Takeaway. Four questions decide the answer. Size, pricing, the moat and the money.


Step Two. Is the Prize Real?
Why believe Anthropic can collect a meaningful slice at all?
Because it already is. The S-1 shows revenue of $16.3 billion in the first half of 2026, up from
$1.2 billion a year earlier. Revenue for the whole of 2025 was $4.6 billion.

Why is it growing so fast?
The money comes mostly from businesses paying per use. Businesses and developers paying
through the API, the connection that lets their software call Claude, brought in about four-fifths
of first-half revenue.
 Slide 51. Businesses paying per use bring in four-fifths of revenue. $13.1B of the $16.3B first-half 2026 total came
                                                  through the API.

Why did businesses start paying?
Coding came first. On 24 February 2025 Anthropic launched Claude Code, a tool that lets
Claude read, edit and test software on its own. The S-1 says its impact was "comparable" to the
arrival of the first popular chat assistants.

Coding suits AI because the result can be checked. The code either runs or it does not, so a
business can see what it is paying for.

Takeaway. The prize is real, and Anthropic is already collecting it from businesses paying
per use.


Step Three. Is AI Priced Like Software or Like Labour?
Why does pricing matter?
A software seat, meaning a flat fee per user per month, caps revenue at the number of people
on staff. Pay-per-use pricing is closer to paying for labour. Revenue then grows with the amount
of work done, not with headcount. That is the only route to a meaningful share of $40 trillion.

Why not just sell more subscriptions?
Because flat fees are expensive to serve. The deck estimates that subscriptions use about 42%
of Anthropic's computing power while bringing in only 20% of revenue. Heavy users on a flat
plan cost far more than they pay.
   Slide 55. Subscriptions use 42% of compute for 20% of revenue. Flat fees reward the heaviest users, not the
                                                   company.

Why might the per-use model also cap revenue?
Because Anthropic keeps cutting its own price. On 22 September 2026 it released Claude Opus
5.5, and the S-1 says the model cut customer costs per task by about 40% compared with Opus
5. The company argues that cheaper tasks drive more usage.

That may be true, but it also means some of the value goes back to customers as lower prices
rather than to Anthropic as revenue.

Takeaway. Pricing per job lets revenue grow with work done, but every price cut hands
some value back to customers.


Step Four. What Can't Be Copied, and Who Else Takes a
Cut?
Copying and the Gap
Why can't a rival simply match Claude?
Some already come close. In January 2025 the Chinese lab DeepSeek released a cheap,
openly available model, and Nvidia lost about $589 billion of market value in one day as
investors questioned whether frontier AI could stay expensive.

"Open models" are ones whose inner settings are published so anyone can run them.
     Slide 30. Open models trail the leaders by about four months, so any lead has to be renewed constantly.

Why does that four-month gap not erase Anthropic's business?
Because the gap is wider on the hardest, best-paid work. On long coding tasks, the best open
model scores about half of Claude's result.




Slide 32. On long coding tasks the best open model scores about half of Claude's. The lead is real where customers
                                                    pay most.

Why can't rivals close that gap by copying?
They try. In February 2026 Anthropic said three Chinese labs used about 24,000 fake accounts
to run more than 16 million conversations with Claude. This is "distillation", training a cheaper
model on a stronger model's answers.
The S-1 lists it as a risk. Copying answers is cheap. Licensed data is not, as the $1.5 billion
Bartz settlement with authors showed. The S-1 records it as a 2025 charge, and a court
approved it in July 2026.

The Apple Rule
Why did the DeepSeek panic not wreck the leaders' revenue?
The most useful pattern here comes from phones. In 2022 Apple shipped only 18% of the
world's smartphones but took 85% of the industry's operating profit, according to Counterpoint.

Volume went to cheap Android phones. Money went to the brand people trusted most.

AI is splitting the same way. Open models now carry about a third of all tokens, the units of text
AI processes, on the router OpenRouter, according to its 100 trillion token study with a16z. Yet
in Menlo Ventures' survey open models account for only 11% of business use, down from 19% a
year earlier.

Hobbyists and startups chase the cheapest token. Companies that pay real money pick the
model they trust with real work.




  Slide 131. The Apple rule. Apple had 18% of phone units and 85% of profit. Open models carry about a third of
                                      tokens but only 11% of business use.

Why does that matter for the $40 trillion?
Because Anthropic does not need to win on volume. It needs to be the model businesses trust
for the costliest work, the way Apple is the phone people pay most for.

Prices Fall Within Months
Why can't Anthropic simply hold a lead once it has one?
Because prices fall within months. On 22 September 2026, the same day Opus 5.5 launched,
Epoch AI published The Plunging Price of Thought. It found that the cost of reaching a fixed
level of AI performance has fallen about 47% a quarter since 2023, or about 13 times a year.

For a skill that has just become possible, the fall is steeper, about two-thirds in the first quarter.




 Slide 43. Prices for a fixed level of skill fall about 47% a quarter on average, and about two-thirds in the first quarter
                                                       after a skill debuts.




       Slide 130. Open models trail by months, not years, so each new Claude buys a short window at the top.

So the premium on a new Claude lasts months, not years. By our rough reading, each release
buys about one quarter at the top before the price of that skill collapses. The Apple rule explains
who gets the money. Falling prices explain why it has to be won again with every release.

Compute and Talent
Why could Anthropic keep winning it with less?
Epoch AI's AI chip users explorer, last updated on 9 September 2026, puts Anthropic at about
1.19 million H100-equivalents of computing power in December 2025, against about 1.74 million
for OpenAI. An H100-equivalent is a common unit that counts all chips as if they were Nvidia's
H100.

Anthropic stayed at the frontier on about two-thirds of OpenAI's compute. The deck's answer is
focus on coding and business work, the tasks that pay. The ranges are wide, and Anthropic has
added capacity since, so treat 68% as a December 2025 snapshot.




  Slide 66. Anthropic reached the frontier on about two-thirds of OpenAI's compute. Epoch medians for December
                                              2025, the latest available.

The other reason is people. Zeki Data, cited by Fortune, counts 22 research hires for every
departure at Anthropic in 2025, against 5.7 at OpenAI. The recruiting firm SignalFire found that
engineers move from OpenAI to Anthropic about eight times more often than the reverse.

Its talent lead, Heather Doshay, says the draw is the mission and safety culture, not pay.
 Slide 77. Anthropic hires 22 researchers for every one who leaves, against 5.7 at OpenAI. The mission, not pay, is
                                                      the draw.

Trust as the Moat
Why does the mission matter to revenue?
Because trust is part of what businesses buy. In February 2026 the President directed federal
agencies to stop using Claude, after Anthropic refused to allow its models to be used for mass
domestic surveillance or fully autonomous weapons. The S-1 records that the Department of
War then labelled Anthropic a "supply-chain risk to national security".

A district court in California largely ruled for Anthropic on 27 August, finding unlawful retaliation.
On 25 September the federal appeals court in Washington ruled 2 to 1 that one of the
designations was proper. Anthropic has asked the full court to rehear the case.

       Slide 110. Placeholder for slide 110, the Pentagon dispute timeline. No current render was available.

Why would a company turn down its largest possible customer?
Because what it sells is trust. Claude's constitution, the public document that shapes how
Claude behaves, lets Claude act as a "conscientious objector" and refuse requests it judges
unethical, even from Anthropic. No other major lab publishes such a clause.
                          Slide 112. Only Claude's rulebook lets it refuse its own maker.

If the Apple rule holds, the buyers who pay most are the ones who care most about trust.
Outside graders back the claim, within limits. The Future of Life Institute's AI Safety Index for
Summer 2026 gave Anthropic the best grade of any lab, but that grade was only a C+, and no
lab scored higher.

In one July 2026 week, Anthropic measured that about 6% of its research compute went to
safety work, a figure it calls conservative.




   Slide 100. Anthropic scores highest on the FLI safety index, but the top grade is a C+ and no lab reaches a B.

Why should investors care about a lab that improves itself?
Because it cuts both ways. Anthropic's August 2026 Risk Report says 18 technical staff
surveyed reported an average speed-up of about four times from Claude on their own work. The
report adds that the lab as a whole is not yet twice as fast, its own trigger for stronger
safeguards.

On the Vals RSI Index, which tests whether a model can do the research that builds the next
model, Anthropic models hold the top three places.

       Slide 83. Placeholder for slide 83, the Risk Report speed-up chart. No current render was available.




         Slide 86. Anthropic models hold the top three places on the Vals RSI Index of AI research skill.

That speed is the product's edge, and also its risk. Under version 3.0 of its Responsible Scaling
Policy, the rulebook for when to pause, Anthropic narrowed the promise to stop. TIME reported
in February that it dropped its pledge never to train a model unless its safety measures were
proven adequate in advance.

Why does the mission bend?
Because the company sells AI workers while warning about AI's effect on workers. In May 2025
Dario Amodei, the chief executive, warned that AI could wipe out half of entry-level office jobs.
On 12 September 2026 he called on the industry to pace the frontier, and ten days later
Anthropic shipped Opus 5.5, the top model.

The company says pacing does not mean stopping. Customers and investors can decide how
much the irony costs.

            Slide 22. Placeholder for slide 22, the mission tensions. No current render was available.
One more cost sits in the S-1. The prospectus says concerns about model welfare, meaning
whether a model's experience matters, may lead Anthropic to delay retiring older models. It
already keeps the weights of retired models and lets Claude end abusive chats.

Anthropic's working hypothesis is that a settled model works more reliably. That is a bet, not a
finding, and it has a price in computing power.




                Slide 108. Anthropic is the only lab with public commitments on model welfare.

So trust works as Anthropic's Apple brand. It lets the company charge a premium for business
work. But it is graded at C+, tested in court, and strained each time the company ships faster
than it says the industry should.

Who Else Takes a Cut
Who else takes a share of each dollar?
Here the comparison with SpaceX, which builds its own rockets and runs its own network, is
useful. Anthropic owns its models but rents most of the chips and data centres it runs on. It also
sells heavily through partners.

The S-1 shows that 47% of 2025 revenue and 48% of first-half 2026 revenue came through
cloud marketplaces, mainly Amazon and Google, which resell Claude and take a cut. So three
groups take money before Anthropic does. They are the clouds that resell and rent, the open
models that push prices down, and customers, who keep savings like the 40% Opus 5.5 cut.

Takeaway. Anthropic wins the profit, not the volume, as long as it stays the trusted brand
and renews its lead every few months.


Step Five. Does the Money Add Up?
Why does any of this matter if revenue is growing?
Because revenue only counts if enough is left after paying for the computing power that serves
it. That leftover share is the gross margin. In 2025 Anthropic kept about 40 cents of each dollar
after serving customers. In the first half of 2026 it kept about 85 cents, by the S-1's own cost
figures.




Slide 54. Anthropic keeps 44 to 88 cents of each dollar after serving customers, depending on what is counted. The
                                S-1's own costs give about 85 cents in early 2026.

Why was 2025 so painful?
Total costs were $12.6 billion against $4.6 billion of revenue, or about $2.76 spent for every
dollar earned. That included the $1.5 billion book settlement.
                        Slide 61. In 2025 Anthropic spent $2.76 for every dollar it earned.

Why does 2026 look so different?
Revenue grew faster than costs. The operating loss shrank to $693 million in the first half, and
day-to-day operations brought in $3.9 billion of cash. The headline net loss of $80.7 billion is
mostly a $72.4 billion non-cash accounting charge on the value of financing, not money spent.




Slide 132. The prospectus shows compute now costs less than revenue, and most of the headline loss is non-cash.

Why worry, then?
Because the next bill is far bigger. By our own sum of the deals the S-1 describes, Anthropic
has signed about $637 billion of compute. About $431 billion must be paid whatever happens,
with Microsoft, Google, Amazon and Broadcom.

About $206 billion is conditional, including a SpaceXAI deal that can be cancelled from early
2027. The S-1 warns that margins may shrink as this new capacity comes online before the
revenue it supports.




              Slide 63. Signed compute equals more than nine years of revenue at today's pace.




             Slide 70. Most of the $637B in signed compute must be paid whatever demand does.

If the money adds up, why did the price talk fall?
On 15 October 2026 Reuters reported that investors had cut their talk of Anthropic's listing value
from about $2 trillion to about $1.5 trillion.
The reason is that growth slowed. The S-1 shows quarterly revenue of $4.73 billion in the first
quarter of 2026 and $11.56 billion in the second, a jump of 144% in one quarter. The S-1 does
not report the third quarter.

Reuters reported on 14 October that it was about $16.8 billion, up 45%. Reuters Breakingviews
noted that a price war on tokens lifted use but cut revenue per unit, the same price collapse
Epoch measured.




 Slide 125. Quarterly growth slowed from 144% to 45%, and price talk fell from about $2T to about $1.5T. The chart
                                         rounds the S-1's 144% to 147%.

Investors price Anthropic on growth, not on today's revenue. Growth of 45% a quarter is still
extraordinary, but a $2 trillion price assumed the faster pace would last. Every quarter from here
is a vote on the valuation.

Takeaway. The money now adds up per dollar served, but $431 billion of firm compute
and slowing growth make the price a bet on speed.


Rebuilding the Answer
Put the parts back together. The prize is real, and Anthropic needs less than 1% of it. Pricing
per use, not per seat, lets it grow with the amount of work AI does.

The Apple rule says the money goes to the most trusted brand, not the biggest volume, and
Anthropic is that brand for business today. But each lead lasts months, so it must be won again
with every release, and clouds and customers take their share along the way. Efficiency in
compute and talent lowers the cost of winning it again.
The money now adds up on a gross basis, but $431 billion of firm compute is owed before the
revenue that justifies it has arrived, and growth has slowed from 144% to 45% a quarter.

So the honest answer is that Anthropic can probably get paid for a few hundred billion dollars of
the world's knowledge work, but only if four things hold together. It must keep the trust that
earns the Apple premium, renew its lead each quarter, keep selling per job rather than per seat,
and grow fast enough to cover compute it has already promised to buy.

A $2 trillion price assumed all four. The S-1 shows the first three are working today. The fourth is
the bet, and the market has already marked it down to $1.5 trillion.
